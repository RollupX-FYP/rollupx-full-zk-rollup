
\section{Component Interactions}
\label{sec:component_interactions}

The modular framework is organized around clearly defined component boundaries. Each component performs a specific role in the ZK-rollup benchmarking pipeline and communicates with the next stage through a controlled interface. This separation improves observability and allows individual stages, such as sequencing, execution, proving, and submission, to be evaluated independently. Table~\ref{tab:component_responsibility} summarizes the implementation language and core responsibility of each component.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Component Responsibility Summary}
\label{tab:component_responsibility}
\compacttable
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.17\textwidth}Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Language} & \textbf{Core responsibility} \\
\midrule
Workload Generator & Python & Generates controlled synthetic transaction traffic using seeded Poisson arrivals and supports repeatable benchmark workloads. \\
\addlinespace

Sequencer & Rust & Receives, validates, orders, and batches Layer-2 transactions using configurable scheduling and sealing policies. \\
\addlinespace

Executor & Rust & Receives sealed batches through gRPC, applies the transfer-centric state-transition function, updates the Sparse Merkle Tree state, and invokes the prover subsystem. \\
\addlinespace

Prover & Rust & Generates proof artifacts using either benchmark-mode mock delays or the \texttt{risc0\_prover} backend for selected real-proof experiments. \\
\addlinespace

Submitter & Rust & Formats the executed batch for the selected data availability mode, manages Layer-1 submission, estimates gas, and handles retry logic through a Saga-style workflow. \\
\addlinespace

Smart Contracts & Solidity & Provide the Layer-1 bridge logic for validating data availability commitments, verifying proofs through \texttt{MockVerifier} or Groth16, and updating the canonical rollup state root. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}
\normalsize

The interaction pattern follows a staged pipeline. The Workload Generator submits synthetic transactions to the Sequencer, which validates and groups them into sealed batches. Once a batch is formed, it is sent to the Executor through gRPC. The Executor applies the state-transition logic, records an execution trace, and invokes the configured prover backend. The resulting payload, which includes the updated state commitment, data availability commitment, and proof artifact, is then streamed to the Submitter. Finally, the Submitter prepares the Layer-1 transaction, estimates the required gas, and broadcasts the batch update to the local Ethereum smart contracts.

This separation of responsibilities is important for the research objective of the project. Because each component is independently observable, the framework can isolate how specific design choices affect overall rollup behavior. For example, the Sequencer can be reconfigured to test different batching or scheduling policies, while the Submitter can be reconfigured to compare calldata, EIP-4844 blob-based submission, and off-chain DA stubs under the same workload conditions.

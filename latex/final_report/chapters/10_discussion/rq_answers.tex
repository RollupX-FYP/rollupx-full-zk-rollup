\section{Answering the Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq_answers}
The following sections address each research question posed at the beginning of the study, mapping the empirical results to the original inquiries regarding batch size, sequencing policies, data availability, and implementation bottlenecks under the finalized system architecture.
\subsection{RQ1: Impact of Batch Size on Performance and Cost}
Batch size dictates the core throughput–latency–cost trade-off. Larger batches significantly reduce the per-transaction L1 gas cost by amortizing the fixed batch settlement and proof verification overheads over a greater number of transactions. However, this economic efficiency comes at the direct expense of latency, as transactions must wait longer in the sequencer queue before the batch is sealed. Furthermore, when using the real RISC0 prover backend, larger batches also reduce the amortized proof generation overhead per transaction, mitigating the massive 70-80s fixed compilation delay by distributing it over more transactions.
\subsection{RQ2: Impact of Sequencer Scheduling Policies}
The experiments evaluated the performance of the Nonce-Aware Priority Scheduler implemented in \path{policies.rs}, which groups transactions by sender account and sorts them strictly by nonce before applying priority heuristics. This design ensures that transactions are processed in strict nonce order, preventing out-of-order execution rejections. The benchmark runs validated this design, achieving 100\% transaction execution success and FCFS-equivalent regular gas costs (~20.2k gas/tx) for both FeePriority and TimeBoost. TimeBoost further improved latency fairness under burst loads, achieving the highest Jain's Fairness Index (0.77).
\subsection{RQ3: Cost Implications of Data Availability Mechanisms}
Data Availability mode selection is the most significant lever for reducing L1 operational costs. Using EIP-4844 blobs instead of standard calldata resulted in a ~70\% reduction in per-transaction costs. Transitioning to off-chain DA further reduced costs by approximately 82.5\% compared to calldata. However, off-chain DA shifts the security assumptions, meaning cost savings must be weighed against the loss of Ethereum's native cryptographic data guarantees.
\subsection{RQ4: Practical Bottlenecks in Implementation}
Implementing the modular prototype revealed several practical engineering bottlenecks. The most prominent bottleneck is cryptographic proof generation; real proving introduces massive fixed overhead (~70-80s setup) and step-function cycle limits governed by segment boundaries ($2^{20}$ cycles) that increase proving time by ~140-150s per segment. Furthermore, relying on local Hardhat RPC for rapid, automated submissions highlighted the volatility of L1 finality and the necessity of robust retry mechanisms (implemented via the Submitter's Saga pattern). Finally, the disconnect between the sequencer's pessimistic cache and the executor's strict canonical state highlighted the difficulty of maintaining state synchronization across decoupled microservices.

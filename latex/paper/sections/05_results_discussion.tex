\section{Empirical Evaluation and Analysis}



\subsection{Data Availability Mode Economics}

To optimize data posting costs, we compare three Data Availability (DA) modes: Calldata, EIP-4844 Blobs, and Off-chain DA. We model the rollup L1 submission cost per transaction ($C_{\text{tx}}$) as:

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{tx}} = \left(R_{\text{gas}} \cdot G_{\text{base}} + \text{DA\_Gas}_{\text{tx}}\right) \cdot P_{\text{ETH}}
\end{equation}

where $R_{\text{gas}}$ represents the fixed execution overhead, $G_{\text{base}}$ is the base fee, and $\text{DA\_Gas}_{\text{tx}}$ is the variable cost. Our baseline market reflects post-Dencun parameters: $P_{\text{ETH}} = \$3,000$, regular gas base fee reference $G_{\text{base}} = 10.0$ gwei, and blob gas fee reference $G_{\text{blob}} = 1.0$ gwei.

\label{fig:da_cost_economics}
\end{figure}


Under Calldata DA, transaction payloads are posted directly to L1 execution space, where variable calldata gas fees (charged per byte) scale linearly with batch size. Consequently, variable posting fees dominate the total batch cost, resulting in a flat per-transaction cost curve that offers negligible economies of scale as batch size increases (see Table~\ref{tab:results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:da_economics}).

In contrast, Blob DA (EIP-4844) and Off-chain DA break this linear scaling relationship. Their L1 footprints are dominated by fixed execution overheads (validity proof verification and state root updates) that remain virtually constant regardless of transaction count. Consequently, scaling the average batch size amortizes these fixed costs over a larger transaction pool, yielding exponential cost-amortization curves (as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:da_cost_economics}). Under high throughput, this modular separation of execution and data availability dramatically reduces amortized transaction costs compared to monolithic calldata architectures.



\subsection{Batch Policy Trade-offs (Fixed vs. Adaptive)}
We analyze the cost-latency trade-offs of Fixed-size versus adaptive batching policies under varying transaction workloads (see Fig.~\ref{fig:policy_comparison}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plot_batch_policy_comparison}
\caption{Batch Policy Comparison (Fixed vs. Adaptive) across Mempool Latency and L1 Gas Cost.}
\label{fig:policy_comparison}
\end{figure}

The adaptive policy monitors the L2 mempool queue depth $d$ to dynamically calculate the target batch size $N(d)$ using the piecewise formulation:
\begin{equation}
N(d) = \begin{cases} N_{\min}, & d < L \\ N_{\text{mid}}, & L \le d < H \\ N_{\max}, & d \ge H \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $L$ and $H$ are depth thresholds, and $N_{\min}$, $N_{\text{mid}}$, and $N_{\max}$ are respective target sizes. A size-triggered seal occurs when $d \ge N(d)$ (producing a batch of size $N_{\text{actual}} = \min(d, N(d))$), while a time-triggered flush occurs when the oldest transaction's wait time $W_{\text{oldest}} \ge T_{\max}$. Sizing logic requires the parameter constraint $N_{\min} < L$.

Consequently, under \textbf{Low Load}, adaptive policy resolves the elevated queue latency of the Fixed policy (which must wait for $T_{\max}$ to expire) by scaling the target down to $N_{\min}$, reducing average wait times at the cost of higher per-transaction L1 gas from smaller batches. Under \textbf{Medium Load}, the adaptive policy converges to near-optimal gas amortization. Under \textbf{High Load}, where L1 block times throttle fixed-batch L1 confirmation throughput, the adaptive policy scales the target to $N_{\max}$ to maximize amortization while maintaining stable queue dynamics.

\subsection{Sequencer Scheduling Comparison}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plot_scheduling_policy_comparison}
	\caption{Sequencer Scheduling Policies Comparison (Normal Load).}
	\label{fig:scheduling_comparison}
\end{figure}

Under steady load, sequencer scheduling policies (FCFS, FairBFT, FeePriority, TimeBoost) yield near-identical wait times and gas costs, as the transaction arrival rate is comfortably below the sequencing capacity. 

However, under burst congestion, policies diverge. \textbf{TimeBoost} and \textbf{FeePriority} reduce average mempool wait times relative to FCFS and FairBFT. By prioritizing high-fee transactions, these schedulers allow urgent payloads to bypass the queue backlog, validating the prioritization mechanisms under high-load spikes.

\subsection{zkVM Proving and Overhead Scaling}
We profile off-chain execution and validity proof generation using the RISC0 zkVM (see Fig.~\ref{fig:prover_scaling}). Because proving is dominated by segment counts and recursion overhead rather than instruction count, wall-clock proving duration scales as a step-function governed by segment boundaries. Despite this non-linear behavior, proving exhibits a strong economy of scale modeled by $T(N) = A + B \cdot N$, where $A$ is the fixed setup overhead and $B$ is the marginal proving cost per transaction. Fixed overhead $A$ dominates at small batch sizes, whereas larger batches amortize these setup costs, driving down the per-transaction proving cost.

However, validity proof generation acts as a hard finality bottleneck. Under continuous workloads, if the proving duration exceeds the batch accumulation interval, a proving queue backlog accumulates, throttling committed L2-to-L1 finality throughput even though L2 execution itself is rapid.
